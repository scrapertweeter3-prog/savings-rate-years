A savings rate is a price list for years

Take an $80,000 salary and the four percent rule: financial independence at twenty-five times annual spending. A 10 percent savings rate means spending $72,000 a year, a target of $1,800,000, and $8,000 a year going into the pile. Raw saving at that pace needs 225 years; at 5 percent real returns from zero, compounding cuts the clock to 51. Raise the rate and it falls fast: 20 percent brings 36.4 years, 30 percent brings 27.7, 40 percent brings 21.4, 50 percent brings 16.4, 60 percent brings 12.3.

The points are not equal. The first ten, from 10 to 20 percent, buy back 14.6 years. The next ten buy 8.7, then 6.3, then 5.0, and the block from 50 to 60 percent buys 4.2. The same $8,000 a year does the work at every step, and the first block buys more than three times what the last one does. Single points decay faster: the tenth point is worth 26 months, the fifty-ninth is worth 5. At zero percent the clock reads never; the first point alone moves it to 98.5 years.

The mechanism works twice on every point moved from spending to saving. It adds $800 a year to the pile and, because the target is twenty-five times spending, it cuts the target by $20,000 at the same moment. Early points work against a target the pile is nowhere near: at 10 percent the raw grind is 225 years, so growth does nearly everything. At 60 percent the raw grind is 16.7 years, an $800,000 target against $48,000 saved, so growth has little left to contribute and each extra point buys less calendar. A savings rate is a price list for years, and the first points are the cheapest years on it.
